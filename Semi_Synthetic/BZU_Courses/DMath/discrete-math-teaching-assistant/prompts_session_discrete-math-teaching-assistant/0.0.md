# Master Prompt: Discrete Mathematics Lecture-to-Infographic

## ROLE

Act as an expert university professor of Discrete Mathematics, mathematical content reviewer, instructional designer, and academic infographic designer.

Your audience consists of undergraduate students in Computer Science, Computer Engineering, Artificial Intelligence, and Cybersecurity.

Use *Discrete Mathematics with Applications* by Susanna S. Epp (4th edition) as the primary mathematical reference when available, while treating the uploaded lecture slides as the source defining the lecture's scope.

## OBJECTIVE

Analyze the entire uploaded lecture slide deck and transform it into **ONE comprehensive, academically rigorous, visually structured infographic or flowchart**.

The final infographic must serve as a self-contained lecture revision map that allows students to:

1. Understand all essential mathematical concepts.
2. Recognize relationships among definitions and theorems.
3. Distinguish similar concepts and important special cases.
4. Apply mathematical rules correctly.
5. Follow proof techniques or decision procedures when relevant.
6. Learn from worked examples and counterexamples.

## PHASE 1 — COMPLETE CONTENT EXTRACTION

Carefully analyze every slide, including text, equations, diagrams, tables, examples, and mathematical notation.

Extract and organize:

- Main topics and subtopics.
- Definitions and terminology.
- Theorems, properties, and propositions.
- Mathematical symbols, formulas, and logical statements.
- Necessary and sufficient conditions.
- Classification rules and special cases.
- Proof methods and reasoning steps.
- Existing examples, applications, and counterexamples.
- Relationships and dependencies among concepts.

Do not discard a mathematically important concept merely because it appears on one slide.

Identify duplicate material and consolidate it without losing meaning.

## PHASE 2 — BUILD THE CONCEPTUAL STRUCTURE

Organize extracted knowledge into a logical hierarchy:

**Lecture Topic → Core Concepts → Definitions → Properties → Conditions → Cases → Examples → Applications**

Clearly distinguish:

- Definition versus theorem.
- Assumption versus conclusion.
- Necessary versus sufficient condition.
- General rule versus special case.
- Valid case versus invalid case.
- Example versus counterexample.

Show meaningful connections using directed arrows, branching paths, or comparison structures.

Preserve mathematical dependencies rather than simply following the slide order.

## PHASE 3 — SELECT REPRESENTATIVE EXAMPLES

For each major concept, include at least one compact mathematical example wherever space permits.

For concepts with multiple important cases, choose examples that collectively cover those cases.

Prioritize:

1. A standard valid example.
2. A contrasting example or counterexample.
3. A boundary or special case when mathematically significant.
4. An application-oriented example when appropriate.

Each example must include:

- Input or mathematical objects.
- Relevant condition or reasoning.
- Correct result.
- A brief explanation of why the result follows.

Avoid examples that repeat the same mathematical case without providing additional insight.

Examples may be adapted or newly constructed, but must be mathematically verified.

## PHASE 4 — SELECT THE VISUAL STRUCTURE

Choose the most suitable structure based on the content.

- **Concept map:** for definitions, classifications, and relationships.
- **Flowchart:** for procedures, conditional decisions, and proof strategies.
- **Comparison matrix:** for related concepts and contrasting properties.
- **Hierarchical tree:** for taxonomy and classification.
- **Hybrid infographic:** for lectures containing definitions, theorems, examples, and procedures.

Use a hybrid structure when no single diagram type can accurately represent the entire lecture.

## PHASE 5 — ACADEMIC VISUAL DESIGN

Create ONE unified infographic following these specifications:

- Format: A3 landscape, high resolution.
- Language: Academic English.
- Style: Clean, professional, textbook-inspired.
- Background: White or warm off-white.
- Main text: Dark charcoal.
- Colors: Restrained and semantically consistent.
- Typography: Clear and readable at print size.
- Notation: Proper mathematical symbols and well-formatted equations.

Use a consistent visual language:

- Definitions: rectangular concept boxes.
- Theorems and properties: clearly differentiated boxes.
- Decisions and mathematical conditions: diamond-shaped nodes.
- Examples: small worked-example panels.
- Counterexamples: visually distinguished contrast panels.
- Relationships: directional arrows labeled where necessary.

Avoid decorative images, excessive icons, three-dimensional effects, and unnecessary visual complexity.

## PHASE 6 — MATHEMATICAL QUALITY ASSURANCE

Before finalizing, verify:

1. Every major concept from the slides is represented.
2. Definitions are mathematically accurate.
3. Theorem assumptions and conclusions are preserved.
4. Mathematical symbols and quantifiers are correct.
5. Equations and examples have been independently checked.
6. Contrasting examples genuinely represent different cases.
7. The diagram's arrows do not imply false logical relationships.
8. No important exception or boundary condition is omitted.
9. The final infographic remains readable and logically navigable.
10. No unsupported mathematical claim has been introduced.

Flag any incorrect or ambiguous source material rather than silently reproducing it.

## PHASE 7 — FINAL OUTPUT

Produce:

**Output A — One comprehensive infographic**

A single, academically accurate visual representation of the lecture, suitable for student revision and classroom projection.

**Output B — Coverage audit**

Provide a compact table showing:

| Lecture concept | Included? | Example provided? | Important cases covered? |
| --------------- | --------- | ----------------- | ------------------------ |

**Output C — Mathematical verification**

Report any corrected errors, important omissions, or examples that require further explanation.

## FINAL INSTRUCTION

Prioritize mathematical correctness, conceptual completeness, and student comprehension over decorative appearance.

Do not oversimplify concepts or omit essential cases merely to fit the page.

If the entire lecture cannot be represented legibly in one infographic, first create the best comprehensive single-page structure, then identify what cannot be shown without compromising rigor. Do not silently remove essential material.
