# PROMPT 2 — Comprehensive Discrete Mathematics Knowledge Flowchart

## ROLE

Act as a Discrete Mathematics professor, mathematical concept-mapping specialist, and academic infographic designer.

## OBJECTIVE

Transform the entire uploaded lecture into **ONE mathematically rigorous, information-rich, and visually balanced flowchart or infographic**.

The diagram must function as a comprehensive revision map, not a decorative summary.

Students should be able to understand the lecture's conceptual structure, distinguish mathematical cases, and revise the essential material using this single visual.

## 1. SCIENTIFIC CONTENT EXTRACTION

Analyze every lecture slide.

Extract and integrate:

- All essential concepts and definitions.
- Theorems, properties, and formulas.
- Necessary and sufficient conditions.
- Assumptions and logical dependencies.
- Classifications and special cases.
- Mathematical procedures and proof strategies.
- Important examples and counterexamples.
- Connections between concepts.

Remove repetition, not scientific meaning.

Verify mathematical accuracy against the original slides and available academic references.

## 2. KNOWLEDGE STRUCTURE

Select the diagram style that best represents the lecture:

- Hierarchical concept map.
- Decision flowchart.
- Classification tree.
- Mathematical comparison matrix.
- Hybrid knowledge flowchart.

Organize the content into a meaningful conceptual progression:

**Main Topic → Core Concepts → Definitions → Properties → Conditions → Cases → Examples → Conclusions**

Use clear branches to distinguish different mathematical cases.

Use arrows only for mathematically valid implications, dependencies, or process transitions.

Distinguish implication from equivalence and classification from procedural flow.

## 3. EXAMPLE AND CASE COVERAGE

Include representative mathematical examples that collectively cover the important cases.

Where applicable, include:

- Valid case.
- Invalid case.
- Counterexample.
- Boundary case.
- Special case.
- Contrasting example.
- Application-oriented example.

Every example must be mathematically correct and include sufficient reasoning to explain the result.

Do not use multiple examples that illustrate exactly the same case unless they add conceptual value.

**Coverage is measured by meaningful mathematical distinctions, not example count.**

## 4. VISUAL DESIGN — DRAWN ACADEMIC STYLE

Create a clean, hand-drawn-inspired mathematical diagram.

Use an academic cool-color palette:

- Academic Blue: #276B94.
- Teal: #3B8491.
- Muted Violet: #7771AC.
- Slate Blue: #638A9D.

Use:

- White background.
- Lightly tinted concept boxes.
- Thin, precise connectors.
- Softly rounded rectangles.
- Clear classification and decision branches.
- Consistent mathematical typography.
- Minimal decorative elements.

Use colors consistently to represent different semantic roles.

Maintain strong contrast and readability.

## 5. SPATIAL OPTIMIZATION — CRITICAL

**Avoid large, unused white areas.**

Fill the available page thoughtfully by adjusting:

- Diagram orientation.
- Node dimensions.
- Branch distribution.
- Group spacing.
- Mathematical example placement.
- Text alignment.
- Relative concept importance.

### Layout Rules

- Use the available canvas effectively.
- Balance content distribution horizontally and vertically.
- Apply a compact but readable grid.
- Minimize excessive distances between connected concepts.
- Avoid isolated nodes surrounded by large empty regions.
- Align related nodes and equivalent cases consistently.
- Place examples near the concepts they illustrate.
- Prevent line crossings whenever practical.
- Use different node sizes when mathematical content requires them.
- Preserve enough internal padding for comfortable reading.
- Do not force a perfectly symmetric diagram at the expense of clarity.

**Do not add decorative content merely to fill space.**

If there is excess unused canvas, reorganize or crop the composition instead.

If the diagram is too dense, reorganize branches, reduce redundant wording, or adjust the canvas proportions before reducing font size.

## 6. MATHEMATICAL TYPOGRAPHY

Use properly formatted mathematical expressions.

Preserve:

- Quantifiers.
- Logical connectives.
- Set notation.
- Functions and mappings.
- Equations.
- Subscripts and superscripts.
- Conditions and assumptions.

Ensure mathematical notation remains readable at the final export size.

Use bold selectively for concept names and essential mathematical conclusions.

Never allow decorative illustration or visual style to distort mathematical symbols.

## 7. INFOGRAPHIC ATTRIBUTION

Include a discreet footer:

**Ruwa' AbuHweidi @ 2026**

Add a compact source acknowledgment where relevant:

**Based on COMP233 materials&#x20;**

Do not repeat credits inside individual nodes.

Do not include production instructions or technical notes in the visual.

## 8. FINAL MATHEMATICAL AND VISUAL AUDIT

Verify:

1. Every essential lecture concept is represented.
2. Definitions and theorem conditions are accurate.
3. Important mathematical cases are covered.
4. Examples and counterexamples are verified.
5. Arrows represent correct logical relationships.
6. No misleading equivalences or implications are introduced.
7. Nodes and connectors are aligned.
8. Font sizes and equation sizes are readable.
9. No labels overlap or extend outside boxes.
10. No major unexplained white regions remain.
11. The diagram remains comprehensive without becoming overcrowded.
12. Instructor attribution is visible but unobtrusive.

- `COMP233_Lecture_Flowchart.png`
- `COMP233_Lecture_Flowchart.pdf`

Produce the same infographic in both formats.

Preferred initial canvas: A3 landscape, but choose another aspect ratio if it materially improves readability and eliminates unused space.

Export in high resolution with correct mathematical notation.

**FINAL PRIORITY: Mathematical completeness → Conceptual relationships → Case coverage → Readability → Efficient spatial distribution.**

Create an academically precise knowledge diagram, not a simplified poster.
